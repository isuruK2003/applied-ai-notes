Introduction to artificial intelligence

Overview

Artificial intelligence (AI) is the field concerned with understanding and building intelligent entities (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 1). This topic covers how AI is defined, how it relates to machine learning, its history, its current state and applications, and its risks and benefits.

Defining artificial intelligence

DefinitionIntelligence. “The ability to learn and perform suitable techniques to solve problems and achieve goals, appropriate to the context, in an uncertain, ever-varying world” (Christopher Manning) (Manning, 2020).
DefinitionArtificial intelligence. “The science and engineering of making intelligent machines” (John McCarthy, who coined the term in 1955 for the 1956 Dartmouth workshop) (McCarthy, 2007).
  • Definitions differ along two dimensions:
    • human versus rational: fidelity to human performance, or an abstract standard of doing the “right thing”;
    • thought versus behaviour: internal reasoning, or externally observable behaviour (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 1).

The four approaches to AI

Acting humanly: the Turing test

  • Proposed by Alan Turing (1950): a computer passes if a human interrogator cannot tell whether written answers come from a person or a computer.
  • Requires natural language processing, knowledge representation, automated reasoning and machine learning.
  • The total Turing test adds physical interaction, so also requires computer vision and speech recognition, and robotics.
  • Researchers have spent little effort on passing it, preferring to study underlying principles (as flight succeeded once engineers stopped imitating birds) (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 2).

Thinking humanly: cognitive modelling

  • Requires knowing how humans think: introspection, psychological experiments, brain imaging.
  • A program whose input–output behaviour matches humans' may share their mechanisms (Newell and Simon's General Problem Solver) (Newell and Simon, 1961).
  • Cognitive science combines AI models with experimental psychology.

Thinking rationally: the “laws of thought”

  • Aristotle's syllogisms (“Socrates is a man; all men are mortal; therefore Socrates is mortal”) began the study of logic.
  • The logicist tradition builds intelligent systems on logic, but logic needs certain knowledge; probability handles uncertainty.
  • Rational thought alone does not produce intelligent behaviour (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 3).

Acting rationally: the rational agent

DefinitionRational agent. An agent (something that acts) that acts so as to achieve the best outcome or, under uncertainty, the best expected outcome (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 4).
  • More general than the “laws of thought”: correct inference is only one route to rationality (a reflex from a hot stove can be better than deliberation).
  • More amenable to scientific development, because rationality is mathematically well defined.
  • The standard model: AI studies agents that do the right thing, defined by the objective given to them.
  • Limited rationality: acting appropriately when there is not time for every computation, since perfect rationality is infeasible (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 4).

Beneficial machines

DefinitionValue alignment problem. The problem of ensuring that the objective put into a machine agrees with true human preferences (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 5).
  • Real-world objectives are hard to specify fully (a self-driving car must trade progress against risk) (Russell and Norvig, 2021, pp. 4–5).
  • Russell and Norvig (2021, p. 5) propose provably beneficial machines: they pursue human objectives while uncertain of them, so act cautiously and defer to humans.

AI, machine learning and deep learning

  • The fields are nested:
    • AI: the broadest field, covering symbolic methods (logic, search) and statistical methods (probability, learning);
    • machine learning: improving performance from experience; many AI systems, such as search algorithms, do not use it (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 1);
    • deep learning: machine learning with many layers of adjustable computing elements (deep neural networks) (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 26);
    • large language models: very large deep networks trained on text to predict the next token (ChatGPT, Gemini, Llama).
DefinitionMachine learning (Mitchell, 1997, p. 2). A program learns from experience E with respect to tasks T and performance measure P if its performance at T, as measured by P, improves with E.
  • Spam filter: T is classifying emails, P is the proportion classified correctly, E is emails labelled by people.
  • Symbolic AI: explicit symbols manipulated by inference or search (physical symbol system hypothesis; Logic Theorist, expert systems) (Newell and Simon, 1976).
  • Connectionist AI: networks of simple units learning from examples (McCulloch–Pitts neuron to deep learning) (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 24).
  • Neurosymbolic AI: combines neural learning with symbolic reasoning.

History of AI

Inception and early enthusiasm (1943–1969)

  • 1943: McCulloch and Pitts's on/off artificial neurons, the first work in AI (McCulloch and Pitts, 1943).
  • 1949: Hebbian learning; 1950: Turing's “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” (Hebb, 1949) (Turing, 1950).
  • 1956: Dartmouth workshop adopts the term AI; Newell and Simon's Logic Theorist proves theorems from Principia Mathematica (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 18).
  • “Look, Ma, no hands!” era: General Problem Solver, Gelernter's Geometry Theorem Prover, Samuel's checkers (early reinforcement learning), Lisp (1958), resolution (1965), perceptrons (Russell and Norvig, 2021, pp. 18–21).
  • ELIZA (1966) imitated a psychotherapist by pattern-matching: conversation, not reasoning (Weizenbaum, 1966).

A dose of reality (1966–1973)

  • Systems relied on introspection rather than analysis of the task.
  • Combinatorial explosion: methods from microworlds did not scale (Russell and Norvig, 2021, pp. 20–21).
  • Minsky and Papert's Perceptrons (1969) showed single-layer limits; neural network funding collapsed (Minsky and Papert, 1969).
  • The Lighthill report (1973) led Britain to end AI support in all but two universities (Lighthill, 1973).

Expert systems and the AI winter (1969–1993)

  • Domain knowledge replaced general “weak methods”: DENDRAL (1969), MYCIN (about 450 rules, certainty factors), R1 (first commercial success) (Buchanan, Sutherland and Feigenbaum, 1969) (McDermott, 1982).
  • Industry grew to billions by 1988, then the AI winter (about 1988–1993): expert systems were hard to maintain, brittle under uncertainty and unable to learn (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 24).

Probability, data and deep learning (1986–present)

  • Back-propagation reinvented in the mid-1980s, reviving neural networks (Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams, 1986).
  • Probabilistic reasoning and machine learning: hidden Markov models, Pearl's Bayesian networks (1988) (Pearl, 1988).
  • Big data (2001–): ImageNet (2009); Watson wins Jeopardy! (2011) (Deng et al., 2009) (Ferrucci et al., 2010).
  • Search-based Deep Blue beats world chess champion Kasparov (1997) (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 30).
  • Deep learning (2011–): Hinton's group wins ImageNet 2012; AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol (2016) and Ke Jie (2017) (Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton, 2012) (Google DeepMind, no date a) (Agence France-Presse, 2017).
  • Overall: cycles of boom and winter; a shift from logic to probability and from hand-crafted knowledge to learning.

Sub-fields and kinds of AI

  • Main sub-fields: problem solving and search; knowledge representation and reasoning; machine learning; natural language processing; robotics and perception; planning and decision making; uncertainty and probabilistic models; ethics and safety.
DefinitionWeak AI. The hypothesis that machines could act as if they were intelligent (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 981).
DefinitionStrong AI. The hypothesis (Searle, 1980) that machines acting intelligently are actually consciously thinking; now often used to mean human-level AI (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 981).
DefinitionArtificial general intelligence. A machine able to learn to do anything a human can do, as opposed to narrow AI, which performs a specific task (Manning, 2020).

Recent developments and drivers

  • Leading organisations: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind (merged 2023), Meta, Microsoft and others (Hassabis, 2023).
  • Language models: ChatGPT, Gemini, open-weight Llama; often multimodal; no evidence they are conscious (Butlin et al., 2023); can hallucinate, reproduce bias and err in multi-step reasoning.
  • Code: GitHub Copilot, Cursor, Claude Code; they assist developers rather than replace them.
  • Media generation: DALL·E 3, Stable Diffusion, Veo (video), Lyria (music), Nano Banana (image editing) (OpenAI, 2023) (Rombach et al., 2022) (Google DeepMind, 2025) (Google DeepMind, no date b) (Fortin et al., 2025).
  • World models: Genie 3 generates interactive environments from text (Parker-Holder and Fruchter, 2025).
  • Games: AlphaZero learned Go, chess and shogi by self-play alone; AlphaStar reached Grandmaster level at StarCraft II (Silver et al., 2018) (Vinyals et al., 2019).
  • Robotics: Boston Dynamics' BigDog and Atlas; low-cost humanoids such as the Unitree G1 (Raibert et al., 2008) (Unitree Robotics, no date).
  • Drivers of progress, applied mostly to decades-old techniques:
    • big data from the Web and devices (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 26);
    • computing power: graphics and tensor processing units; training compute rose 300,000-fold from 2012 to 2018 (Amodei and Hernandez, 2018);
    • algorithmic advances such as the transformer (2017) (Vaswani et al., 2017).

Applications and hype

  • Industry: healthcare (diagnosis from images), services (chatbots), energy (demand prediction), finance (fraud detection), life sciences (drug discovery), telecommunications (network optimisation).
  • Medical AI on average matches health-care professionals at image diagnosis; adoption depends on clinical outcomes, transparency and privacy (Liu et al., 2019).
  • Agentic AI: systems, usually built on LLMs, that pursue goals over many steps using tools.
  • Gartner's hype cycle: innovation trigger, peak of inflated expectations, trough of disillusionment, slope of enlightenment, plateau of productivity.
    • 2025: generative AI in the trough (at the peak in 2023); AI agents at the peak; AGI at the innovation trigger, more than ten years away (Gartner, 2023) (Gartner, 2025).
  • Golden age: real-world results across many industries, not toy domains.
  • Caution: success comes mainly from data and compute; predictions have repeatedly been premature; investment can outrun the science (PwC estimates up to $15.7 trillion added to the global economy by 2030) (PwC, 2017).

Risks and benefits of AI

Benefits

  • Insight into complex problems and faster science (disease, climate).
  • Freeing people from menial, repetitive work (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 31).
  • Learned skills copied instantly between machines.
  • Aiding human creativity, a “bicycle for the mind”.

Near-term risks

  • Unclear responsibility when systems cause harm.
  • Lethal autonomous weapons, a concern because of their scalability (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 31).
  • Mass surveillance and political persuasion.
  • Biased decisions from biased data (a recruiting model that favoured men) (Dastin, 2018).
  • Effects on employment; wealth shifting from labour to capital.
  • Safety-critical applications that are hard to verify; cybersecurity threats (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 32).

Long-term risks

DefinitionTechnological singularity. The hypothesised point at which computers reach superhuman performance and then rapidly improve themselves further (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 12).
  • Artificial superintelligence: intelligence far beyond human ability.
  • Gorilla problem: humans could lose control to a more intelligent entity, as gorillas have.
  • King Midas problem: a machine may achieve exactly the objective given, with unintended consequences (Russell and Norvig, 2021, p. 33).
  • Proposed remedy: provably beneficial machines, uncertain about human objectives and therefore deferring to humans (Russell and Norvig, 2021, pp. 33–34).

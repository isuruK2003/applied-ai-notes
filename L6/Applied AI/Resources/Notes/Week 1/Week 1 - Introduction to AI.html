Introduction to artificial intelligence

Overview

What intelligence and artificial intelligence mean, how AI developed through periods of excitement and disappointment, where it stands today, how industry uses it, and what people hope for and worry about.

What is artificial intelligence?

How the main areas fit together

Artificial intelligence is the widest term. Machine learning is the part of AI where computers learn patterns from data instead of following rules written by hand. Deep learning is the part of machine learning that uses neural networks with many layers. Large language models, such as the ones behind ChatGPT, are one kind of deep learning model.

The part of AI outside machine learning is the older symbolic approach. It works with explicit rules, logic and search. Machine learning, by contrast, is statistical: it deals in probabilities learned from examples.

Definitions to know

Intelligence
“The ability to learn and perform suitable techniques to solve problems and achieve goals, appropriate to the context, in an uncertain, ever-varying world.” (Professor Chris Manning, Stanford)
Artificial intelligence
The term was coined by John McCarthy in 1955. He defined it as “the science and engineering of making intelligent machines”.
Key pointMcCarthy kept his definition deliberately open, to encourage people to explore. That helped the field grow, but it also led to promises that could not be kept. The same tension shows up in today's arguments about what counts as “artificial general intelligence”, meaning AI that matches people across every kind of task.
Good to knowThe Lighthill report. This 1973 report was written for the UK government's science research council. It argued that AI had failed to deliver on its big promises, mainly because real problems involve far too many possibilities to search through. UK funding for AI was cut sharply afterwards, and the report is often linked with the start of the first AI winter.
Machine learning
Tom Mitchell's definition: “A computer program is said to learn from experience E with respect to some class of tasks T and performance measure P, if its performance at tasks in T, as measured by P, improves with experience E.”
Good to knowMitchell's definition in practice. For a spam filter, the task T is sorting emails into spam and not spam, the performance measure P is the percentage sorted correctly, and the experience E is a collection of emails that people have already labelled.

A short history of AI

  • 1940–1950The early days
    • 1943: Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts describe the brain as a circuit of simple on/off units. This is the first model of an artificial neuron.
    • 1950: Alan Turing publishes “Computing Machinery and Intelligence”, which introduces the Turing Test.
  • 1950–1970Early excitement
    • 1950s: the first AI programs appear, including Arthur Samuel's checkers player, Newell and Simon's Logic Theorist and Gelernter's geometry program.
    • 1956: a workshop at Dartmouth College adopts the name “artificial intelligence”.
    • 1965: John Alan Robinson publishes a complete method for logical reasoning.
  • 1970–1990Knowledge-based systems
    • 1969–1979: the first systems built around large collections of expert knowledge.
    • 1980–1988: the expert systems industry booms.
  • 1988–1993The expert systems industry collapses: an “AI winter”
  • 1990–2012Statistics and specialist areas
    • Probability comes back, with a focus on handling uncertainty.
    • Each area of AI becomes much more technically deep.
    • Work on agents and learning systems grows, and people talk of an “AI spring”.
  • 2012–todayExcitement again
    • Large amounts of data, powerful computers and neural networks come together.
    • Separate areas of AI start to merge again.
    • AI is used across many industries.

AI then and now

Comparing the most common words in AI writing from 1986–1990 with those from 2020 onwards shows how the field has changed. The data comes from aitopics.org. In the late 1980s the most common terms included artificial intelligence, neural network, machine learning, expert system, knowledge, representation, algorithm and logic programming. From 2020 onwards they include machine learning, deep learning, natural language, dataset, algorithm, large language model, reinforcement learning, transformer and chatbot.

Key pointTwo traditions. Symbolic AI works with explicit symbols, rules and logic. Examples are the Logic Theorist, expert systems and search. Connectionist AI learns from data using networks of simple connected units, from McCulloch and Pitts' neuron to today's deep learning. Neurosymbolic AI tries to combine the two.

Recent developments

Companies to watch

OpenAI, Anthropic, Hugging Face, Google DeepMind, Tesla and xAI, Microsoft, Meta, Amazon and IBM.

Google DeepMind was formed in April 2023, when Google merged its two main research groups, Google Brain and DeepMind. One recent example of its work is Genie 3, which generates interactive virtual worlds.

Chatbots and language models

  • ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Perplexity and many open-source models.
  • Many can now work with images and sound as well as text.
  • They raise open questions: could they be conscious, and what are their limits?

Writing code from plain English

  • Software development involves much more than writing code, such as working out what users need, designing systems and testing them.
  • These tools mostly make developers more productive rather than replacing them.
  • They are built into coding tools such as GitHub Copilot and Cursor.
  • Competition is intense: Claude Code, OpenAI Codex and Copilot.

Images, video and music from plain English

  • OpenAI's DALL·E 3.
  • Stable Diffusion, which is open source.
  • Google DeepMind's Veo for video, Lyria for music and Nano Banana for editing images.

Games

  • AlphaZero plays chess, shogi and Go.
  • DeepMind's systems play 57 Atari games better than people.
  • AI beats top human players at StarCraft II.

Robots

  • Humanoid robots such as the Unitree G1, and the latest work from Boston Dynamics.

Why AI is advancing so fast

Two reasons stand out:

  • Data: there is a huge amount of text, images and video to learn from.
  • Computing power: specialised chips and very large data centres, with more being built.
Good to knowTwo other reasons are often given. One is better methods, such as the Transformer design for neural networks published in 2017. The other is openness: free software, shared models and research papers published freely online.

A golden age, or another AI winter?

Gartner's “hype cycle” for AI shows how expectations of different AI technologies changed between 2023 and 2025. A hype cycle tracks how expectations of a new technology rise and fall over time. It has five stages: a breakthrough sets things off, expectations peak, disappointment follows, understanding slowly improves, and finally the technology becomes genuinely useful.

The arguments

  • There is a lot of hype, but this time results hold up on real problems, not just simplified test cases.
  • Most of the recent success comes from more computing power and more data. Many of the techniques are decades old.
  • AI is always “the next big thing” and so never quite arrives. Elon Musk has promised self-driving cars “next year” for many years. Should we call it “computational intelligence” instead, to calm the hype?
  • Money and marketing can run ahead of the science. PwC estimates that AI could add about $15.7 trillion to the world economy by 2030.
Good to knowA correction. The slides say that Gartner predicts AI will be a $16 trillion industry by 2030. In fact the figure comes from PwC, and it is an estimate of how much AI could add to the world economy, not the size of the AI industry.

Concerns and benefits

Concerns

  • Responsibility: when AI makes a harmful decision, who is legally and morally responsible?
  • Jobs: AI may replace people, including in creative work.
  • The singularity: what happens if AI becomes smarter than people? Geoffrey Hinton, Elon Musk, Ray Kurzweil and others have given their views.
  • Consciousness: can AI be conscious? Is general AI possible at all, and would it try to take control?
  • Mind uploading: could a human mind be copied into a machine?
  • Bias: AI trained on past hiring decisions, for example, has favoured men.

Benefits

  • Insight: AI can help us understand very complex problems, much as AlphaGo showed expert Go players new ideas rather than ruining the game.
  • Work people cannot do: machines can work around the clock, raising productivity and quality of life.
  • Instant learning: a skill can be copied to another machine at once, where people need years.
  • Help for human creativity: AI can be a “bicycle for the mind”.
  • War without soldiers: fewer people might be put at risk, although this cuts both ways.

Question 1Multiple choice

Which early program showed that a computer could reason logically?

The Logic Theorist, written by Newell and Simon in the 1950s, proved theorems from Principia Mathematica. ELIZA was a chatbot, the General Problem Solver came later, and expert systems belong to the 1980s.
Question 2Multiple choice

Who came up with the term “artificial intelligence”, and when?

John McCarthy came up with it in 1955, ahead of the 1956 Dartmouth workshop. He defined it as “the science and engineering of making intelligent machines”.
Question 3Multiple choice

In Tom Mitchell's definition of learning, what is the performance measure P for a spam filter?

P measures how well the program does. The task T is sorting the emails, and the experience E is the collection of labelled emails.
Question 4Multiple choice

Which of these correctly shows how the areas fit inside each other?

AI is the widest area, machine learning is part of AI, and deep learning is part of machine learning. Large language models are part of deep learning.
Question 5Multiple choice

What did McCulloch and Pitts propose in 1943?

Their model of an artificial neuron is the starting point of the tradition that leads to today's neural networks.
Question 6Multiple choice

What caused the AI winter of roughly 1988 to 1993?

Expert systems boomed from 1980 to 1988. They were expensive, broke easily outside their narrow area and had been over-promised, so when the industry collapsed the funding went with it.
Question 7Multiple choice

What explains most of AI's recent success?

Many of the techniques are decades old. What changed is the amount of data available and the computing power to learn from it.
Question 8Multiple choice

In Gartner's 2025 hype cycle for AI, where is generative AI?

Generative AI was at the peak in 2023 and had fallen into the disappointment stage by 2025. AI agents replaced it at the peak.
